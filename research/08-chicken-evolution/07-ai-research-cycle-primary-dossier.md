# Chicken evolution: a primary-source dossier and inquiry map

**Evidence horizon:** 5 August 2026  
**Scope:** the lineage leading from deep animal ancestry to living chickens; the fossil transformations on the dinosaur–bird stem; crown-bird, galliform, and *Gallus* relationships; domestication and interspecific gene flow; selected conservative archaeological dispersal anchors; and the questions that remain decision-useful.  
**Source rule:** original research, new fossil descriptions, genomic analyses, direct archaeological studies, and formal technical replies/reanalyses. DOI links lead to the primary records. No installed Eido Profile or private board was inspected.

This is a tree only where descent is tree-like. Chicken history becomes a **network** inside *Gallus* because species exchanged genes, and it becomes a **human–animal process** during domestication because management, movement, selection, and backcrossing happened at different times. Treating all three histories as one ladder creates false precision.

## Executive synthesis

1. A chicken is a living crown bird, a galloanseran, a galliform, a phasianid, and a member of *Gallus*. Its invariant deep route runs through Archosauria, the bird-line archosaurs, Dinosauria, Theropoda, Coelurosauria, Maniraptora, Paraves, and Avialae. Some names and node definitions between those landmarks vary among phylogenetic conventions.
2. Fossils such as *Teleocrater*, *Archaeopteryx*, *Zhengheornis*, *Baminornis*, *Navaornis*, *Asteriornis*, *Vegavis*, and *Gallinuloides* are best treated as side relatives that reveal character combinations—not as a chain of direct chicken ancestors.
3. A major 2026 update is *Zhengheornis buyu*: a Late Jurassic avialan with 15 shortened caudal vertebrae but no pygostyle. It makes tail reduction before terminal fusion the best-supported stepwise model. The 2025 claim that *Baminornis zhenghensis* possessed the oldest known avialan pygostyle remains anatomically disputed.
4. Crown-bird timing is model-sensitive. A large family-level genomic analysis estimated crown birds near 93 Ma and Galloanserae near 68 Ma, whereas morphology-plus-fossilized-birth–death tip dating placed crown birds around 100 Ma. Other genomic-clock work favors more sustained Late Cretaceous diversification. These chronologies should be compared as whole models, never spliced into a synthetic timeline.
5. The well-supported galliform family ladder is **Megapodiidae first, then Cracidae, then Numididae, then Odontophoridae + Phasianidae**. Exact divergence dates are clock estimates, not fossil observations.
6. Two complete, incompatible genome-scale roots for living *Gallus* remain in play. Tiley and colleagues place green junglefowl first; Mariadassou and colleagues place red junglefowl first. Both recover grey and Sri Lankan junglefowl as sisters. The root conflict matters for genus evolution, but it does not overturn the evidence that domestic chickens derive most ancestry from red junglefowl.
7. Of sampled living red-junglefowl populations, *Gallus gallus spadiceus* is the closest principal source ancestry for domestic chickens. The reported 9,500 ± 3,300-year split is a demographic model estimate—not a dated corral, a domestication event, an archaeological specimen, or proof of one exact founder locality.
8. Domestic chickens are genomic mosaics. Later inputs came from other red-junglefowl subspecies and candidate tracts from grey, Sri Lankan, and green junglefowl. Most admixture statistics do not establish direction or literal genome-wide ancestry percentages. The clearest phenotype-specific case is yellow skin at **BCO2** (historically written **BCDO2**), with grey-junglefowl ancestry and a newly reported 24-bp regulatory insertion as a candidate causal variant.
9. The earliest currently auditable, unambiguous chicken assemblage in the broad archaeological synthesis is Ban Non Wat, northeast Thailand, associated with a phase dated about 1650–1250 BCE. The bones themselves were not directly dated, and domestic status is contextual. Earlier northern Chinese and several Yunnan claims are respectively refuted or still unauditable; they must not be allowed to set the lower bound.
10. Dispersal was not equivalent to dietary adoption. Direct dating in Europe and Northwest Africa shows arrival in the Iron Age and, in many contexts, a delay of centuries before routine consumption. New biomolecular studies in Central Asia, Japan, Korea, Norway, and the Baltic expose different local pathways—egg production, successive breeding, managed feeding, display/ritual value, and later meat use.

## How to read the evidence

### Five kinds of date that must not be merged

| Date label | What it dates | Example | What it does **not** date |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fossil occurrence** | A preserved organism or fossil-bearing horizon | *Asteriornis*, 66.8–66.7 Ma | The exact origin of its clade |
| **Molecular-clock estimate** | A node under sequence, calibration, and clock assumptions | Galloanserae near 68 Ma in one genomic clock | A directly observed ancestor or event |
| **Demographic split estimate** | Separation in a population model, often with continuing gene flow | domestic–*spadiceus* split around 9.5 ± 3.3 ka | Archaeological domestication or first husbandry |
| **Associated phase/context** | The dated stratum or cultural phase containing a specimen | Ban Non Wat, about 1650–1250 BCE | A direct radiocarbon date on the bone |
| **Direct specimen date** | The sampled object itself, usually AMS radiocarbon | Karako-Kagi chicken, 381–204 BCE | The first chicken anywhere in the region |

### Inference hygiene

- A fossil sister branch is not a direct ancestor.
- A named crown clade and a similarly used stem/total clade are not automatically synonyms. In particular, **Aves/Neornithes**, **Euornithes/Ornithuromorpha**, and **Sauria/Diapsida** vary by author and definition.
- Present-day geographic proximity is not a domestication location.
- A demographic divergence is not a domestication date.
- A layer date is not a direct bone date.
- Morphological resemblance, iconography, or an old museum label is not species identification.
- D, *f*, and related admixture statistics are not literal percentages of a modern animal's whole genome unless a method explicitly estimates and validates that quantity.
- A locus with introgressed phenotype ancestry does not imply hybrid origin of the entire species.
- Ancient absence is rarely proof of true absence when recovery, identification, and dating are uneven.

## 1. The nested lineage to the domestic chicken

The following is an operational backbone, not a claim that every intervening rank has equal evidential status. The deeper animal nodes are included for continuity; the annotated evolutionary evidence begins at Archosauria. Broad phylogenomic evidence supports monophyletic Chordata within deuterostomes ([Bourlat et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature05241)), the bony-vertebrate → sarcopterygian → tetrapod spine ([Irisarri et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-017-0240-5)), and turtles as the genomic sister lineage of archosaurs, for which the turtle–archosaur crown was named Archelosauria ([Crawford et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ympev.2014.10.021)). Three qualifications are essential:

1. **Basal dinosaur names are hypothesis-dependent.** A 2017 analysis proposed Ornithoscelida and moved Theropoda relative to Ornithischia ([Baron, Norman & Barrett 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature21700)); a detailed response showed how character coding and sampling alter that result ([Langer et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature24011)). The safe invariant for this dossier is **Dinosauria → Theropoda**, while placement inside a named Saurischia depends on the adopted topology.
2. **“Crown birds” is the intended meaning at the living-bird node.** Many authors call that clade Neornithes; some use Aves for the crown and others use Aves more broadly. The biological branch is clearer than the label.
3. **The *Gallus* children above are an inventory, not a resolved branching order.** The two supported orders are compared in section 5.

## 2. Deep transformation: archosaur to crown bird

### 2.1 The bird line existed before dinosaurs

*Teleocrater rhadinus*, a Middle Triassic aphanosaur, falls near the base of Avemetatarsalia and outside Ornithodira. Its crocodile-like ankle and body proportions show that the earliest known bird-line archosaurs did not already look like small, cursorial dinosaurs. The bird-line package was assembled after the crocodile–bird split rather than appearing at once ([Nesbitt et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature22037)).

**Decision implication:** do not narrate the bird lineage as continuous progressive “birdification.” Early members retained character combinations later divided among crocodile-line and dinosaur-line forms.

### 2.2 Theropod miniaturization was sustained, not sudden

Body-size reconstruction across theropods found sustained miniaturization on the lineage leading to birds, paired with unusually rapid anatomical innovation ([Lee et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1252243)). A separate broad dinosaur analysis also recovered exceptional, long-running size decrease on the avian stem ([Benson et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.1001853)). These analyses make the small-bodied avialan condition a cumulative history, not a one-step consequence of powered flight.

**Unresolved:** the direction of causation among body size, ecology, metabolism, feather functions, and aerial behavior. Correlation along a branch does not identify the initiating selective pressure.

### 2.3 Feathered aerodynamic experiments crossed the avialan boundary

The dromaeosaurid *Microraptor gui* preserved long pennaceous feathers on both forelimbs and hindlimbs, demonstrating a four-surface aerial body plan on a non-avialan paravian branch ([Xu et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature01342)). Late Jurassic *Anchiornis huxleyi* preserved extensive pennaceous feathers, including long metatarsal feathers, before *Archaeopteryx* in time; its exact position among troodontids, early avialans, and nearby paravian branches has varied with analysis ([Hu et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08322)).

These fossils show that complex feathers and enlarged limb surfaces were distributed across neighboring paravian branches. They do not by themselves identify one flight origin, one locomotor route, or the original selective function of feathers.

### 2.4 *Archaeopteryx* is a mosaic, not “the first bird” in a simple ladder

Bone geometry in *Archaeopteryx* is consistent with active flapping but not necessarily the flight stroke of living birds ([Voeten et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-03296-8)). The exceptionally preserved Chicago specimen adds a mosaic palate, previously unknown soft-tissue and feather detail, and evidence for specialized tertiary feathers ([O'Connor et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08912-4)). A 2026 study of the feeding apparatus described avian-like basihyal and oral structures and proposed a functional link to the energetic demands of flight; that causal interpretation remains a hypothesis rather than a measured behavior ([O'Connor et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.xinn.2025.101086)).

**Decision implication:** use *Archaeopteryx* to explore combinations of flight, feeding, and feather traits. Do not use it as a fixed morphological threshold between “dinosaur” and “bird.”

### 2.5 Tail shortening preceded terminal fusion

The 2025 description of *Baminornis zhenghensis* from the Late Jurassic interpreted its terminal caudal structure as the oldest known avialan pygostyle ([Chen et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08410-z)). Subsequent anatomical discussion raised an alternative: the relevant bones may be a synsacrum rather than fused terminal tail vertebrae, a problem explicitly considered in new comparative work on short-tailed birds ([Huang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cretres.2025.106244)). Until the anatomy is re-imaged and independently coded, “oldest pygostyle” should remain a conditional claim.

The July 2026 description of *Zhengheornis buyu* supplies a more secure transition. It had 15 shortened caudal vertebrae but no pygostyle, at an estimated body mass of roughly 74–163 g. The fossil supports a stepwise sequence in which caudal count and length declined before the terminal vertebrae fused ([Wang et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aeb5202)).

**Current best statement:** Late Jurassic avialans had already evolved strong tail reduction; whether a true pygostyle is also securely present that early depends on the disputed identity of the *Baminornis* element.

### 2.6 The Cretaceous had multiple successful bird experiments

Enantiornithines were a diverse Cretaceous radiation outside the living-bird line. The three-dimensionally preserved braincase of *Navaornis hestiae* reveals a brain with a striking mix of ancestral and derived features, narrowing but not simply filling the gap between non-avialan theropods and living birds ([Chiappe et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08114-4)).

This matters for chicken evolution because many traits often narrated as one coordinated modern-bird package—short tail, flight apparatus, brain organization, palate, feeding mechanics—were recombined independently among extinct branches.

### 2.7 The end-Cretaceous filter and the crown-bird radiation

Global forest collapse after the end-Cretaceous impact has been proposed as an ecological filter favoring ground-associated crown-bird survivors, followed by repeated re-evolution of arboreal habits ([Field et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2018.04.062)). This is a testable macroevolutionary model, not a complete account of survival: body size, development, diet, geographic distribution, and sampling remain potential co-causes.

The latest Cretaceous fossil *Asteriornis maastrichtensis* (66.8–66.7 Ma) combines galliform-like and anseriform-like cranial traits and was recovered close to the galloanseran split. It is strong evidence that near-crown galloanserans existed immediately before the K–Pg boundary, but it need not be the first galloanseran or a direct ancestor of chickens ([Field et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-2096-0)).

*Vegavis iaai* illustrates why fossil claims need versioned status:

- Its 2005 description placed it within Anseriformes, implying a Cretaceous radiation of living-bird lineages ([Clarke et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature03150)).
- A 2018 reassessment argued that its crown-waterfowl affinities were not securely demonstrated ([Mayr et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cretres.2018.02.013)).
- A nearly complete skull reported in 2025 restored strong support for waterfowl affinity and crown-bird placement at 69.2–68.4 Ma ([Torres et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-08390-0)).

The safe current wording is **a Late Cretaceous member of the waterfowl total group, with its precise position relative to crown Anseriformes still worth testing**—not simply “the oldest duck.”

## 3. Crown-bird time is a model comparison, not one settled number

### 3.1 Three live chronological pictures

| Model family | Primary result | What makes it valuable | Main caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Family-level genomic coalescent** | Using 363 species representing 218 families and 63,430 intergenic loci, Stiller and colleagues estimated the crown-bird split at about **93.0 Ma** (85.8–100.9) and Galloanserae at about **68.19 Ma** (66.10–70.67). Most neoavian ordinal diversification was concentrated around the K–Pg interval. ([Stiller et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07323-1)) | Dense family representation, many low-homoplasy loci, explicit gene-tree discordance | Node ages inherit fossil-calibration and relaxed-clock assumptions; rapid radiation creates incomplete lineage sorting |
| **Morphology + fossilized-birth–death tip dating** | Crown birds centered around **100 Ma**; an extremely deep Early Cretaceous origin and an origin restricted to the final 10 Myr of the Cretaceous were both rejected. Many major divergences straddle or follow K–Pg. ([Brocklehurst & Field 2024](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2023.2618)) | Fossils enter as dated tips rather than only hard node bounds | Morphological character models, preservation, and fossil placement can dominate uncertainty |
| **Alternative genomic-clock calibration** | A 124-species, 25,460-locus analysis argued for a more continuous Late Cretaceous rise of modern bird diversity rather than a single explosive post-impact pulse. ([Wu et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2319696121)) | Directly tests the “big bang” narrative with a distinct clock design | Alignment, homology, dating strategy, and interpretation were challenged in a formal technical comment ([Springer & Gatesy 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2406494121)); calibration sensitivity was analyzed independently ([Claramunt et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2405887121)) |

These results are not three measurements of the same physical timestamp with random error. They are outputs of different data, fossil placements, rate models, and priors. A decision-useful comparison keeps each model's node ages together and asks whether known fossils satisfy its implied minimum bounds.

### 3.2 What is stable despite the clock debate

- Crown birds divide into Palaeognathae and Neognathae.
- Neognathae divide into Galloanserae and Neoaves.
- Galloanserae divide into Galliformes and Anseriformes.
- Galloanseran lineages were present by the latest Cretaceous, supported by *Asteriornis* and the renewed waterfowl placement of *Vegavis*.
- Much of neoavian ordinal diversification occurred near the K–Pg transition, though “just before,” “across,” and “just after” remain model-dependent.

## 4. Galliformes: the family scaffold and fossil side branches

A 135-taxon ultraconserved-element analysis recovered the same five-family ladder across data-selection strategies ([Chen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12862-021-01935-1)):

```text
Galliformes
├─ Megapodiidae                         mound-builders
└─ remaining galliforms
   ├─ Cracidae                          guans, chachalacas, curassows
   └─ remaining galliforms
      ├─ Numididae                      guineafowl
      └─ remaining galliforms
         ├─ Odontophoridae              New World quail
         └─ Phasianidae                 pheasants, partridges, turkeys, chickens
```

An earlier 88-taxon multigene analysis independently supported this family arrangement ([Wang et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0064312)).

Chen and colleagues' clock gives one internally coherent set of estimates:

| Node | Estimated age, Ma (95% interval) |
|---|---:|
| Anseriformes–Galliformes | 82.1 (69.5–96.5) |
| crown Galliformes | 71.7 (60.1–84.2) |
| Cracidae + remaining galliforms | 62.4 (52.0–73.7) |
| Numididae + remaining galliforms | 41.3 (35.4–47.8) |
| Odontophoridae–Phasianidae | 39.9 (34.0–45.9) |
| crown Phasianidae | 35.4 (30.3–40.6) |

These are **UCE-clock estimates**. The substantially older galloanseran number than Stiller et al.'s estimate is a concrete example of calibration and model dependence, not an error to average away.

The early Eocene *Gallinuloides wyomingensis* is a stem galliform and helps polarize characters near the crown, but it is not a crown phasianid and not an ancestral chicken ([Ksepka 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1096-0031.2009.00250.x)).

## 5. *Gallus*: one stable relationship and one unresolved root

Living *Gallus* contains four species:

- *G. gallus*, red junglefowl, including the domestic lineage;
- *G. varius*, green junglefowl;
- *G. sonneratii*, grey junglefowl;
- *G. lafayettii*, Sri Lankan or Ceylon junglefowl.

Genome-scale work uses the consistently recovered *Bambusicola* sister relationship to root *Gallus* ([Tiley et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40657-020-00194-w)). Two original calibrated galliform analyses place the genera's split around 15–16 Ma and the earliest divergence among living *Gallus* roughly 4–5 Ma ([Hosner et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2017.0210); [Wang et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/jbi.12782)). Those values are molecular-clock estimates, not dates estimated from Tiley and colleagues' genome matrix.

### Hypothesis G1: green junglefowl first

Using new genomes for the three non-red species, about 32 million aligned bases, 3,406 gene trees, concatenation, and ASTRAL analyses, Tiley and colleagues recovered:

```text
(G. varius, (G. gallus, (G. sonneratii, G. lafayettii)))
```

The topology was stable across exons, introns, ultraconserved elements, and conserved non-exonic elements. Tests also detected ancient gene flow between red and green junglefowl and between red and grey junglefowl. A central limitation was one captive individual for each non-red species ([Tiley et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40657-020-00194-w)).

### Hypothesis G2: red junglefowl first

With 149 samples, more than 35 million SNPs, explicit screening of captive grey-junglefowl hybrids, and two wild Indian grey junglefowl, Mariadassou and colleagues favored:

```text
(G. gallus, (G. varius, (G. sonneratii, G. lafayettii)))
```

They argued that captive admixture and gene flow can distort the apparent root ([Mariadassou et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ympev.2020.107044)).

### The stable and unstable conclusions

| Conclusion | Status | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Grey + Sri Lankan junglefowl are sisters | **Stable** | Recovered by both complete genome-scale hypotheses |
| Red is the principal species source of domestic chicken ancestry | **Stable** | Independent domestication-scale genome sampling; not contingent on the genus root |
| Green or red is the first living *Gallus* branch | **Unresolved** | Different sampling and introgression controls produce different roots |
| The four species form a strictly bifurcating history | **False simplification** | Both analyses detect cross-species gene flow |

The right representation is therefore **two alternative backbones plus reticulation edges**, not a consensus tree that hides the root conflict.

## 6. Domestication is a process layered onto a reticulate genome

### 6.1 Principal red-junglefowl source ancestry

An analysis of 863 genomes spanning all four junglefowl species and five red-junglefowl subspecies found domestic chickens closest to sampled *G. g. spadiceus* populations in southwestern China, northern Thailand, and Myanmar ([Wang et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41422-020-0349-y)). The defensible statement is:

> Among sampled surviving wild populations, *spadiceus* is the principal source ancestry closest to domestic chickens.

It does **not** establish one exact founder village, one surviving population identical to the founder, or one instantaneous domestication. The paper's domestic–*spadiceus* divergence estimate of approximately **9,500 ± 3,300 years ago** is generated by a demographic model. It can predate visible archaeological husbandry because population structure, sampling, bottlenecks, protracted management, and later wild gene flow separate genetic coalescence from a human event.

The same study found later regional contributions from other red-junglefowl subspecies, including *murghi*, *bankiva/gallus*, and *jabouillei*. Domestic chicken ancestry is therefore principally *spadiceus*-like, not exclusively so.

### 6.2 Inputs from other junglefowl species

Across 87 genomes, Lawal and colleagues found candidate introgressed regions involving grey, Sri Lankan, and green junglefowl, while confirming red junglefowl as the major domestic ancestor ([Lawal et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-020-0738-1)). Evidence involving grey junglefowl was often compatible with bidirectional exchange. Closely related donor species, incomplete lineage sorting, linked tracts, and sparse wild sampling make donor identity and arrow direction harder than detecting non-tree history.

Use three confidence levels:

1. **Robust:** domestic chickens are overwhelmingly red-junglefowl-derived, with later interspecific introgression.
2. **Supported candidate:** particular regions resemble grey, Sri Lankan, or green junglefowl ancestry.
3. **Needs locus-specific proof:** direction, timing, adaptive consequence, and causal allele.

### 6.3 Yellow skin is the clearest locus-level example

The yellow-skin phenotype was mapped to a regulatory difference at **BCDO2**, now commonly **BCO2**, with the yellow-skin haplotype derived from grey junglefowl and reduced tissue-specific expression affecting carotenoid deposition ([Eriksson et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1000010)). A 2026 genetic study identified a **24-bp regulatory insertion** in the BCO2 region strongly associated with yellow skin and advanced it as a candidate causal variant ([Rong et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12711-026-01067-4)).

This is a refinement of mechanism, not a reason to infer that the domestic chicken as a whole descends from grey junglefowl.

### 6.4 TSHR is not a clean “domestication mutation”

The derived TSHR state is already at high frequency in sampled *spadiceus*, weakening its use as a unique marker of initial domestication ([Wang et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41422-020-0349-y)). Ancient-DNA frequency modeling instead inferred a strong rise beginning around **920 CE** (95% interval 290–1210 CE), plausibly associated with intensified medieval husbandry rather than first domestication ([Loog et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1093/molbev/msx142)).

The proper question is not “Which gene domesticated the chicken?” but “Which allele changed in frequency under which management regime, in which region, and how securely can causality be separated from demographic turnover?”

### 6.5 Domestic ancestry is also entering wild populations

Comparison of historical museum and modern genomes found substantial and geographically heterogeneous domestic introgression into present-day red junglefowl. Global ancestry models commonly placed domestic contribution around 20–50%, while local-ancestry estimates varied far more and were explicitly method-sensitive ([Wu et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1010551)).

**Conservation consequence:** a modern bird labeled “wild red junglefowl” is not automatically an unadmixed proxy for the ancestral source. Historical specimens, dense geographic sampling, and multiple ancestry methods are necessary.

## 7. Archaeology and dispersal: selected conservative anchors

This is not an exhaustive catalogue of every claimed chicken bone. It privileges specimens or assemblages whose identification, context, and date can be independently audited. The broad reassessment examined evidence from more than 600 sites across 89 countries and explicitly combined zoogeography, morphology, osteometry, stratigraphy, context, iconography, and texts ([Peters et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2121978119)).

### 7.1 Lower bound and early movement

| Place and evidence | Date type and date | What can be concluded | Important limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ban Non Wat, northeast Thailand** | Associated cultural phase, about **1650–1250 BCE** | Earliest currently auditable, unambiguous chicken assemblage in the broad synthesis; consistent with early management in a rice/millet farming setting | Bones are not directly dated; “domestic” is contextual and inferential ([Peters et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2121978119)) |
| **Central China, South Asia, Mesopotamia** | Contexts in the later second millennium BCE | Chickens are securely present by this broad interval | Does not rehabilitate much older northern-China claims ([Peters et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2121978119)) |
| **Melanesia** | Archaeological contexts in a similar later-second-millennium-BCE horizon | Early eastward human transport followed the Southeast Asian appearance | Exact first-arrival route and tempo remain sampling-dependent ([Peters et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2121978119)) |

The proposed commensal pathway—wild junglefowl attracted to rice and millet cultivation, followed by tolerance and management—is plausible and spatially concordant, but it remains a causal hypothesis. Crop presence alone does not prove chicken domestication.

### 7.2 Westward movement and changing human use

Direct radiocarbon dating of 23 chicken bones from 16 European and Northwest African sites placed secure arrivals in the Iron Age and exposed frequent legacy misdating. Early individuals were often complete, old, or specially deposited; routine dietary incorporation followed centuries later in many areas ([Best et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.15184/aqy.2021.90)).

Selected anchors from the combined record:

- **Ethiopia:** directly dated chickens around 800–600 BCE.
- **Mediterranean Europe:** chickens established by roughly 700 BCE; routes include Greek and Phoenician exchange but need not reduce to one corridor.
- **Lower Egypt:** secure evidence in Achaemenid contexts (about 550–330 BCE), with wider intensification in the Ptolemaic period.
- **Upper Rhine/Danube and southeast England:** late sixth to early fifth century BCE; arrival did not immediately make chicken ordinary food.
- **Maresha, Israel:** mass exploitation becomes visible in the Hellenistic fourth–second centuries BCE, including demographic and butchery evidence for food production ([Perry-Gal et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1504236112)).
- **Southern and eastern Africa:** widespread evidence comes more than a millennium after the earliest Ethiopian anchor; Comoros and Madagascar show chickens around 750–900 CE in the broad synthesis.

These dates are regional minimums from available evidence, not a single expanding wave front.

### 7.3 Southern Central Asia: eggshell reveals production behavior

Zooarchaeological mass spectrometry and eggshell microscopy identified chicken eggshell across urban and rural sites in southern Central Asia from the last centuries BCE through the medieval period. The oldest directly dated contexts include Paykend, **515–392 BCE**, and Bash Tepa, **385–206 BCE**. Recurrent, abundant eggshell was interpreted as evidence for frequent egg production and likely out-of-season laying ([Peters et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-46093-2)).

That behavioral inference is stronger than a mere species label but weaker than an ancient-genome result: no causal laying allele or exact founding population was recovered. The paper's prose also alternates between counts of chicken-bearing sites and all examined localities, so the robust claim is **multi-site ubiquity**, not a brittle site total.

### 7.4 East Asia: site-specific pathways, not one regional story

**Japan.** ZooMS identification and direct dating at Karako-Kagi established an immature chicken at **381–204 BCE**. Immaturity in a settlement context supports local successive breeding rather than import of only adult birds ([Eda et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/feart.2023.1104535)). At the separate gateway settlement Karakami, a 2025 ZooMS study identified six mature chickens, five probably male, while all five immature bird bones proved not to be chicken or another galliform. That site therefore provides no evidence for successive breeding despite clear chicken presence ([Eda et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oa.70027)).

**Korea.** ZooMS identified five chickens among 14 phasianid remains at Gungok-ri. Direct dates of **80–361 CE** and enriched nitrogen isotope values support human feeding or management in the Proto-Three Kingdoms period ([Kim et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jasrep.2025.105561)). This is a management inference from biomolecular identification, direct chronology, and diet—not merely a new earliest-date claim.

The contrast between Karako-Kagi and Karakami is especially useful: adjacent regions and similar periods can record local reproduction at one place and mostly mature, perhaps transported or socially selected birds at another.

### 7.5 Northern Europe: late arrival, heterogeneous value and ancestry

At medieval Borgund in Norway, direct and associated dates place the earliest secure chicken around **887–986 CE**. Mature age profiles and depositional context favor eggs, feathers, display, or social value over intensive meat production; the transport route remains unresolved ([Walker et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/oa.70018)).

A 2026 Baltic study compared mitochondrial and phenotype-linked markers in 113 ancient bones and 103 modern landrace feathers. Nearly all typed ancient birds belonged to mitochondrial haplogroup E1, but one Åland specimen with haplogroup B dated directly to **266–541 CE**, the earliest direct Finnish chicken and the earliest non-E1 ancient European specimen in that dataset. Ancient frequencies at TSHR and BCDO2 were heterogeneous; modern Finnish haplogroup A likely reflects later input ([Olli et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41437-026-00842-9)). Small, nonrandom samples and limited amplification make this a regional signal, not a complete population history.

### 7.6 The Pacific and the South American claim

Authenticated ancient Pacific chicken mitochondrial DNA supports a distinctive ancestral Pacific haplogroup D and showed that some previously reported haplogroup-E sequences were compatible with modern contamination. The study found no diagnostic Polynesian chicken haplotype in the tested early South American material ([Thomson et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1320412111)).

The original El Arenal 1 report used morphology, direct dating, and mitochondrial DNA to argue for pre-Columbian Polynesian chickens in Chile ([Storey et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0703993104)). A later reassessment found the morphology of 66 purported chicken remains consistent with tinamous; DNA from three candidate bones also identified tinamou, and the originally dated specimen could not be relocated for retesting ([Buhring et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/15564894.2023.2268551)).

**Narrow conclusion:** El Arenal 1 no longer substantiates a pre-Columbian chicken in South America. This does not by itself decide every proposed form of pre-Columbian trans-Pacific contact.

## 8. Early claims that should be refuted, quarantined, or reformulated

### 8.1 Northern China, early Holocene: refuted as chicken evidence

An ancient-DNA paper proposed early Holocene chicken domestication in northern China ([Xiang et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1411882111)). Reanalysis of 1,831 bird bones, including 429 specimens previously labeled domestic chicken, found none that could be retained as chicken; the few plausible galliform candidates occurred at only two of 18 sites and postdated the early-middle Holocene ([Eda et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2016.01.012)). Ecological and zooarchaeological reassessment independently challenged the feasibility and identifications ([Peters et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.quascirev.2016.04.004)).

**Status:** do not use those specimens to date domestication or dispersal.

### 8.2 Yunnan sites: potentially important, not yet auditable

A formal comment argued that Caiyuanzi (reported cultural range 2475–2205 BCE), Dadunzi, and other Yunnan material should push chicken history earlier ([Peng et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2210996119)). The reply noted that the relevant Caiyuanzi remains had not been adequately described or figured, Dadianzi lacked secure morphological confirmation and direct dating, and Dadunzi relied on a single “possibly domesticated” bone ([Peters et al. 2022 reply](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2213678119)).

**Status:** quarantine rather than dismiss. The claims become decision-grade if the original specimens are made available for comparative morphology, ZooMS, and direct AMS dating, with contamination-aware DNA attempted only where preservation permits.

### 8.3 “Ban Non Wat proves a single origin”: too strong

Ban Non Wat is a conservative current lower bound for auditable assemblages, not proof that domestication began there. It could be near a management center, downstream from one, or part of a geographically broad process whose earlier material is unsampled or misidentified.

### 8.4 “The genomic split proves domestication began 9,500 years ago”: category error

The 9.5 ± 3.3 ka estimate and the second-millennium-BCE archaeological horizon can both be true. They measure different phenomena with different biases. Only an explicit demographic–archaeological model can connect them.

## 9. An integrated history with confidence boundaries

### High confidence

- Chickens are living theropod dinosaurs nested within crown birds, Galloanserae, Galliformes, Phasianidae, and *Gallus*.
- Tail reduction evolved before terminal caudal fusion; *Zhengheornis* now demonstrates the unfused short-tail stage in the Late Jurassic.
- Latest Cretaceous fossils establish near-crown galloanseran and waterfowl-line birds before the K–Pg boundary.
- The galliform family ladder is Megapodiidae → Cracidae → Numididae → Odontophoridae + Phasianidae.
- Grey and Sri Lankan junglefowl are sister species.
- Domestic chicken ancestry is principally red-junglefowl-derived and closest, among sampled living populations, to *G. g. spadiceus*.
- Later red-subspecies and non-red junglefowl introgression contributed to domestic diversity.
- Ban Non Wat is the earliest auditable unambiguous assemblage in the current global reassessment, with associated rather than direct dating.
- El Arenal 1 does not presently substantiate a pre-Columbian chicken.

### Medium confidence or model-dependent

- Two focal 2024 analyses favor a crown-bird origin around 93–100 Ma; the recent clock studies disagree more sharply over the ages and tempo of downstream neognath and ordinal divergences, so the chronology remains model-sensitive.
- Two calibrated galliform analyses place the earliest living-*Gallus* split around 4–5 Ma and the *Gallus–Bambusicola* split around 15–16 Ma.
- Rice/millet farming enabled an early commensal pathway in mainland Southeast Asia.
- Frequent Central Asian eggshell records imply sustained egg laying and perhaps out-of-season production.
- Enriched isotopes at Gungok-ri imply managed feeding.

### Open or currently conditional

- Whether *Baminornis* possesses a genuine pygostyle.
- Whether green or red junglefowl is sister to all other living *Gallus*.
- The precise wild population(s), place(s), and duration of initial chicken domestication.
- The direction, timing, and adaptive effect of most non-red-junglefowl tracts.
- Whether unaudited early Yunnan remains are genuinely chicken and securely dated.
- Exact routes into many regions, including Scandinavia, the Baltic, Africa, and individual Pacific island chains.

## 11. Questions designed for Eido Ask AI

These are bounded, evidence-auditing prompts. Each should return claims with source, date type, assumptions, and a confidence label.

1. **Clock integrity:** “Build three internally coherent crown-bird chronologies from Stiller 2024, Brocklehurst & Field 2024, and Wu 2024. Do not mix node ages across models. For each chronology, test *Vegavis* and *Asteriornis* as fossil minimums and list any tension.”
2. **Tail-character audit:** “Create a character matrix for *Archaeopteryx*, *Zhengheornis*, *Baminornis*, Enantiornithes, and crown birds: caudal count, proportional tail length, terminal fusion, feather fan, synsacrum identity, and preservation confidence. Which single recoding changes the inferred order of tail evolution?”
3. **Fossil versus ancestor:** “For every named fossil in this dossier, state whether the paper places it on a stem, total group, crown, or unresolved branch. Rewrite any sentence that implies direct ancestry.”

5. **Introgression arrows:** “For every non-red-junglefowl tract in Lawal 2020, distinguish evidence for admixture, donor identity, direction, timing, and phenotype. Mark any arrow that is inferred only from modern samples.”
6. **BCO2 causal chain:** “Trace the yellow-skin claim from grey-junglefowl haplotype to tissue-specific expression to the 2026 24-bp insertion. Which link is association, which is functional evidence, and which still needs an intervention experiment?”
7. **TSHR reframe:** “Explain how a derived TSHR state can be common in *spadiceus* yet rise under medieval husbandry. Produce at least three demographic or selective models that fit both observations.”
8. **Genetic–archaeological interval:** “Show how a 9.5 ± 3.3 ka demographic split and a 1650–1250 BCE archaeological lower bound can coexist. Separate sampling gap, protracted domestication, population structure, and model misspecification.”
9. **Ban Non Wat downgrade:** “Re-run the origin narrative with Ban Non Wat coded as: (A) domestic, (B) managed but not morphologically domestic, (C) wild commensal, and (D) misidentified. Which conclusions survive all four states?”

## 12. Conversation arcs for deeper exploration

### Conversation A — When did a chicken become a chicken?

1. Define four thresholds: genetic divergence, sustained human management, reproductive isolation from wild birds, and visible archaeological domestic phenotype.
2. Ask which threshold the user means by “domestication.”
3. Map Wang 2020 and Ban Non Wat to different thresholds.
4. Test whether a protracted, geographically distributed process explains both.
5. End with the ancient samples that would falsify that model.

**Productive tension:** the answer changes because the term changes, not because one discipline is wrong.

### Conversation B — Is a lineage still a tree after gene flow?

1. Start with the two *Gallus* backbones.
2. Add red–green and red–grey reticulation edges.
3. Add domestic backcrossing into wild red junglefowl.
4. Ask which tasks still require a tree and which require a network.
5. Compare a species tree, a local ancestry graph, and a management history.

**Productive tension:** no one visualization answers species branching, locus ancestry, and human movement simultaneously.

### Conversation C — What did early people value?

1. Compare first presence with first routine butchery.
2. Use Europe, Maresha, Central Asian eggshell, Japanese age profiles, Korean isotopes, and Borgund age structure.
3. Generate competing models: sacred/exotic animal, fighting/display, egg producer, mobile food reserve, meat stock.
4. Specify what age, sex, pathology, isotope, eggshell, and depositional pattern each predicts.
5. Rank sites where a new measurement would discriminate among models.

**Productive tension:** “domestic” is not synonymous with “food.”

### Conversation D — How much confidence belongs in an oldest-known claim?

1. Compare *Baminornis*, Ban Non Wat, Karako-Kagi, Gungok-ri, Åland, and El Arenal.
2. Label identification method and date type for each.
3. Remove one evidence link at a time.
4. Observe which “oldest” claims survive.
5. Rewrite results as minimum bounds rather than origin dates.

**Productive tension:** the strongest oldest-known claims join direct identity and direct date on the same specimen; many famous claims join neither.

| Variation axis | State A | State B | Optional state C | Downstream cards to recompute |

| *Gallus* root | Green-first (Tiley) | Red-first (Mariadassou) | Network with no forced bifurcating root | ancestral traits, biogeography, introgression arrows |

| Early chicken value | Eggs | Display/ritual | Meat/mixed | age/sex predictions, site comparison, dispersal incentives |

For every variation, preserve three outputs:

1. **What changes?** The claims downstream of that assumption.
2. **What survives?** Conclusions invariant across all states.
3. **What would decide?** The cheapest authoritative new observation.

## 14. A compact evidence schema for future additions

Every new card or claim can use this structure:

```yaml
claim: one falsifiable sentence
entity: taxon, specimen, site, population, gene, or node
relationship: ancestry, sisterhood, introgression, presence, management, or phenotype
date_value: numeric interval if applicable
date_type: fossil | molecular_clock | demographic | associated_phase | direct_AMS
identity_method: morphology | CT | ZooMS | mtDNA | nuclear_DNA | context | combined
source: DOI to primary paper
sample_scope: number, geography, wild/captive/ancient, preservation
directionality: demonstrated | modeled | ambiguous | not_applicable
confidence: high | medium | conditional | refuted
live_alternative: strongest competing explanation
decisive_test: observation most likely to change the decision
```

This schema prevents an old label, a layer date, a modern haplotype, and a causal phenotype experiment from appearing as equivalent evidence.

## 15. Fresh deltas visible by 5 August 2026

- **Late Jurassic tail evolution changed materially:** *Zhengheornis* provides secure short-tail-before-fusion evidence, while *Baminornis* should now be displayed with a bone-identity warning rather than as an unconditional oldest pygostyle.
- **The *Archaeopteryx* mosaic expanded:** the Chicago specimen and 2026 feeding-apparatus work add palate, feather, tongue-support, and oral characters without turning *Archaeopteryx* into a direct ancestor.
- **Yellow-skin mechanism sharpened:** a 24-bp regulatory insertion at BCO2 is now a candidate causal variant layered onto the older grey-junglefowl introgression result.
- **East Asian husbandry gained direct biomolecular anchors:** Japan now has contrasting site-level breeding signals, and Gungok-ri combines ZooMS, direct dates, and isotope evidence for managed chickens in Korea.
- **Northern European history became more heterogeneous:** Borgund supports late, non-intensive-meat use, while ancient Baltic haplotypes and phenotype-linked alleles show more diversity than a one-line east-to-west replacement narrative.
- **The South American chicken claim narrowed further:** the El Arenal material available for reassessment is tinamou, and the original dated specimen is unavailable. The result withdraws that chicken datum without adjudicating all contact questions.
- **The central methodological delta is representational:** a defensible chicken history now needs a nested tree, competing clock/topology variants, introgression edges, and an evidence-typed human chronology. A single smooth timeline is less accurate than the uncertainties it conceals.

## Bottom line

The modern chicken is the surviving tip of a deep theropod branch whose bird-like characters accumulated mosaically, not in a straight march. Its nearest history is even less tree-like: four *Gallus* species diverged and exchanged genes; red junglefowl supplied the overwhelming domestic background; *spadiceus*-like populations are the closest surviving sampled source; other junglefowl contributed particular tracts; and domestic ancestry now flows back into “wild” populations.

Archaeology supplies a different clock. The best current lower bound is an associated second-millennium-BCE assemblage in mainland Southeast Asia, followed by regionally uneven transport and radically different human uses. The most important unanswered questions are therefore not requests for one older date. They are requests to join the right kind of evidence to the right claim: direct anatomy for *Baminornis*, geographically representative wild and ancient genomes for the *Gallus* root and domestication source, direct identification plus direct dating for early sites, and local behavioral evidence for what transported chickens were actually for.
