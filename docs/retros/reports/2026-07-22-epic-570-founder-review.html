Epic 570 — Bounded Rich-Card Renderer: Founder Review

Executive summary

The full S0–S8 renderer train landed on main on 2026-07-18 and the epic closed on 2026-07-19: Eido's Reading view now keeps rich Card DOM bounded to what is near your viewport (never more than 540 Cards), instead of keeping every rich Card on the whole Board resident. Fidelity survived — search, copy, keyboard access, long-Card editing, and saved-Board compatibility all shipped with direct tests. The critical honesty boundary: the performance benefit was proven structurally (bounded counters and set membership), never by a stopwatch — the train contains zero controlled timing measurements, by explicit recorded policy. And your own test — ~30 long-text Note Cards, all on screen — is a scenario this epic deliberately did not make cheaper; the train in fact removed an old cap that used to make exactly that scenario artificially cheap but unreadable.

What changed now

Rich Card DOM no longer grows with whole-Board content. Offscreen Cards release their DOM once outside a 1,350 px retention band; a lightweight painted safety layer guarantees no blank regions; live interactions (editing, selection, focus, media) pin their Cards independently so eviction can never interrupt you. Long Notes are now fully faithful at Reading zoom — a previous board-wide 300,000-character cap that silently demoted everything to the painted Overview on text-heavy Boards was removed (#580).

What users or the business receive later

The payoff scales with Board size: on Boards far larger than one screen, navigation cost stays flat instead of growing with total content. That claim is EXPECTED, not yet timed — the gate is a controlled-host timing run (or honest field feedback), which the train explicitly recorded as not-measured. Your 30-Card lag report (examined in its own section below) is the first field signal, and it points at a cost the epic never bounded: many long rich Cards visible at once.

Your lag report, examined — "laggy after ~30 long Notes"

You tested the delivered renderer expecting to place many large Note Cards on a Board without losing performance, and hit lag at roughly 30 long-text Notes. This section answers, from the shipped code and the train's own evidence record, whether that contradicts the epic — and what the honest diagnosis is.

1. Your scenario is outside what the epic bounded — by design

The delivered residency planner admits visible Cards first, with no cap short of 540 (src/canvas/cardResidency.ts, PASSIVE_CARD_LIMIT = 540). The scene code even carries the comment "Text length never changes this decision." So ~30 long Notes visible at Reading zoom means all ~30 hold full rich DOM simultaneously — exactly as specified. The epic's promise was different: Cards away from your viewport stop costing you. On a Board where most content fits on or near one screen, there is nothing offscreen to bound, so the bounded renderer contributes approximately nothing to that test. DECIDED (spec: visible Cards under existing scene and LOD policy; residency bounds apply to the preload window P, not to V).

2. The train removed the cap that used to make your exact test artificially fast

Before this train, a board-wide 300,000-character text cap demoted every Card to the cheap painted Overview once total Board text crossed it. A real save (the "Alphabet" Board, 307,964 text units) tripped it: one long Note pushed the whole Board out of Reading. Mid-train, #580 deliberately removed that cap so long Notes stay readable and unrelated Cards keep their detail. Consequence for your test: ~30 long Notes is right at the old threshold's scale — under the old behavior your Board would likely have collapsed to painted Overview (fast, but unreadable rich content); under the new behavior it renders everything faithfully and pays the real cost. The product got more honest, and in this one scenario likely heavier. That trade was made consciously for readability, but it was never presented as a performance trade, and no timing accompanied it (#580's browser perf suites were non-blocking and not run). MEASURED (cap removal and its cause), NOT YET PROVEN (its cost in your scenario).

4. So — did the epic actually make an impact?

What is proven: the mechanism landed and its bounds hold structurally (resident-DOM plateaus below the ceiling, zero blank frames, image caches under 8 MiB/2 MiB budgets, owners bounded). What is not proven: that anyone's real session got faster. No wall-clock number exists on either side of the train — the spec itself reserved timing for three narrow claims and then an in-flight policy amendment (ADR 0028) removed even those unless a product decision depended on them. What your test shows: the one scenario the architecture concedes — many long rich Cards visible at once — behaves as the architecture predicts, and may be net-heavier than pre-train because of #580. The honest verdict is: impact on large-Board navigation is ENABLED and EXPECTED; impact on dense visible text is NOT YET PROVEN and plausibly negative; your test measured the second thing, not the first.

Decision for you: should "many long Notes visible at Reading zoom stays fluid" become a named product claim with its own issue and a controlled measurement? The good news: the fixes don't require new architecture — Applying the infrastructure shows the shipped machinery already contains the levers (an inert truncation cap, an unconsulted text-weight signal, a gated-off painted motion path); they need wiring, not invention.

Applying the infrastructure — where the next user-felt gains live

Founder directive for this pass: the foundation is accepted; make sure what was built is actually applied to impactful end-user performance. A code-verified scan of the shipped seams found that the infrastructure is real — and that three of its own protections are currently unused or gated off. These are tuning moves, not new architecture; each one reuses a seam this epic (or its evidence phase) already paid for.

1. Bound long card bodies — the shipped truncation cap is dead code, and no content-visibility exists Highest impact

What exists: a passive-preview truncation helper (markdownPreviewText, 1,200-char cap, src/shared/markdownPreview.ts) written explicitly to protect "auto-sized cards from painting unbounded text," plus contain: layout paint style on note/result bodies. The gap: the helper is called from exactly one place (NoteCard.tsx:35) and both branches return the full text — auto-height notes bypass it entirely, fixed-height notes short-circuit it. There is no content-visibility: auto anywhere in production CSS, and Result bodies even override their 320 px height cap to unbounded. Net: 30 long Notes = 30 complete DOM trees, plus fully-mounted offscreen residents within the 900 px preload window. The tweak: add content-visibility: auto with contain-intrinsic-size from the card's already-measured height to card body wrappers — geometry-stable, so it avoids the flash that originally led to bypassing the truncation cap. Expected user effect: the browser skips rendering work for offscreen-but-mounted bodies; directly attacks the 30-long-note scenario at mount and during navigation. ENABLED today, unapplied.

2. Wire the text-weight signal into a local demotion budget — the #580 successor already exists

What exists: the scene computes a per-card text weight and a board-wide boardDetailTextWeight on every derivation, incrementally and cheaply (boardScene.ts). This is precisely the signal the removed 300k-character global cap used — the removal (#580) deleted the global demotion but the measurement survived. The gap: the value is copied onto the scene and never consulted; every demotion decision keys off card count ("Text length never changes this decision"). The tweak: a per-viewport weight budget in the residency planner — stop admitting full detail once cumulative admitted weight crosses the budget, demoting overflow to the painted layer. Local, so one giant note can't dim an unrelated corner of the Board — fixing the exact flaw that justified removing the old cap, while restoring its protection. Expected user effect: bounded worst-case cost on text-heavy viewports; the "ton of large note cards" use case gets a real ceiling again. ENABLED today, unapplied.

3. Motion-scoped painted demotion — shipped, wired, gated off

What exists: a complete cached-passive presentation path that can show a captured raster instead of live DOM, already receives a navigationActive signal every frame, and already decides 'safety-net' vs 'passive' per card (cachedPassiveCard.ts, wired through Board.tsx). The gap: it only activates behind the build flags of the rejected always-on cached-passive renderer — production never enables it, so during fast pans all visible rich cards stay fully live. The rejection reasons (selection, clipboard, keyboard, accessibility) are all things a user cannot exercise mid-gesture. The tweak: honor the painted path only while navigationActive is true, reverting to live DOM the instant motion settles. This must be framed carefully: it reuses the shipped capture runtime for gestures only — it is not a revival of the rejected renderer, and the settle-back-to-live behavior keeps every fidelity guarantee at rest. Expected user effect: lighter frames during fast pan/zoom on heavy Boards. ENABLED today, gated off; needs a deliberate product decision because the seam was previously litigated.

Selected decision or architecture

The evidence phase (#406) selected buffered rich-DOM residency and terminally rejected the two competitors: strict viewport-only residency (too much churn at edges) and the cached-passive-Card renderer (rejected outright). The selected design keeps the saved graph as sole authority — every runtime set (resident Cards, pins, caches, counters) is derived and rebuildable, honoring ADR 0011. Governing ADRs 0019/0020/0023/0024 were amended to "production since 2026-07-19," and the canvas foundation doc now teaches the shipped model. Eager whole-Board data loading was deliberately kept (#408): the performance problem was DOM residency, not data volume.

Area 2 — Technical teaching

Technical story in one minute: think of the Board as a city and rich Cards as fully furnished rooms. Before, every room in the city stayed furnished forever — walking anywhere got slower as the city grew. Now only the rooms near you (within ~one screen's margin) are furnished; rooms you're actively using are bolted open and can't be unfurnished under you; everywhere else shows an accurate painted facade so the city never looks empty. The trade: furnishing a room you approach costs a moment, and if you stand in a plaza surrounded by thirty huge furnished rooms, they're all real — nothing about this design makes that cheaper.

How it works

Mechanism. A "cold" Board Scene derives, from the graph and viewport alone (never from pointer state), the sets that drive rendering: V visible Cards, P a preload window (Cards within 900 px, kept until 1,350 px away — that gap is the hysteresis that stops flicker at the boundary — plus direction-aware look-ahead while you pan), L Cards pinned by live interaction owners, E ⊆ L active editors, G at most two lightweight gesture proxies, and S the painted safety representation covering everything else. Rich DOM is exactly P ∪ L, capped at 540 Cards; above 540 visible Cards the whole viewport demotes to the painted layer. During motion, one CSS transform moves the whole Card container per frame — individual Cards are not re-rendered. Zoom bands govern representation: Reading (rich DOM) at zoom ≥ 0.325, painted Overview between 0.15 and 0.325, Map pills below 0.15.

Trade-off. Bounded residency buys flat memory and flat navigation cost on large Boards; it pays with preparation cost at the preload boundary and full price for whatever is actually visible. Product meaning. Big Boards stop punishing you for having history; what you're looking at is always real and always complete. Validation gate. Structural: resident/owner/image counters must plateau under their ceilings (asserted in probes, inspectable at runtime via window.__EIDO_PERF__). Wall-clock: still open — no controlled timing exists.

Friction, bottlenecks, and how they were unblocked

Real friction was small and well-handled: S4's first Required run failed its bundle budget by 0.2 KiB — repaired by lazy-loading the search panel chunk rather than raising the budget. Two macOS temp-root alias test failures were correctly classified host-explainable rather than blocking. A Windows WAL canary failure post-train was classified host-sensitive against an identical green tree. S1's review found the one genuine correctness blocker (exported residency sets reflected planner candidates rather than mounted detail) and it was repaired pre-merge. The structural discipline held throughout: budgets were never raised to pass, and unprovable claims were recorded not-measured instead of being faked.

Founder interventions

Two pre-approved founder decisions (2026-07-19) worked exactly as intended: app-level equivalents for Find/copy/accessibility removed a whole class of mid-train escalations, and the no-rollout-apparatus ruling kept slices small. The intervention that deserves scrutiny is the mid-train ADR 0028 evidence amendment: it eliminated redundant ceremony (good) but also absorbed the last reserved timing runs (S1, S7) into "timing only when a product decision depends on it." The product decision that depended on it — "does this feel faster?" — arrived one week later as your lag report. Better timing: land the amendment, but explicitly re-confirm which timing claims survive it, in the epic thread, before the affected slices merge.

Agent and team accountability

Three things the agents should have caught without being asked. First, the routing mandate said codex-implemented slices get Opus 4.8 review; when Opus authentication expired on S6, the recorded substitution was a same-vendor Codex Sol review — the substitution was logged, which is honest, but the mandate's cross-vendor invariant was broken for the rest of the train and nobody circled back. Second, #580 changed the performance envelope of text-heavy Boards (removing a global demotion cap) inside a performance epic, with its own perf suites marked non-blocking and not run — that deserved one sentence of founder-visible flagging: "long-Note Boards will now pay real rendering cost." Third, the epic closed without a retro document; the founder-review scaffold sat empty until this session. Delivery speed was excellent; the reflective layer lagged.
